All numbers below are from results_8k.csv (Tesla T4, 8K, 30 degrees, nearest, block 16x16).

SLIDE 2 (0:20)
An 8K frame has 33 million pixels, and each output pixel depends only on the input, so the task is data parallel. We use inverse mapping: each thread owns one output pixel and applies R transpose to find its source, so no holes and no write races.

SLIDE 3 (0:35)
cudaMalloc reserves both buffers in GDDR6. For host to device, the GPU's DMA copy engine pulls the bytes over PCIe. From pageable memory, the driver first copies into a pinned staging buffer. cudaMallocHost gives page-locked memory, so the DMA reads it directly and async copies can overlap. The kernel works only in GDDR6, then device to host brings the result back. PCIe is the narrow pipe: pinned copies ran at 12.3 gigabytes per second, against 320 inside the T4, and pageable memory was 2.7 times slower.

SLIDE 4 (0:25)
The host allocates, copies, picks the launch configuration, launches the kernel asynchronously, then synchronises and checks errors. The device runs thousands of threads in SIMT style. Our block is 16 by 16, so 256 threads, or 8 warps of 32. For 8K the grid is 480 by 270 blocks.

SLIDE 5, code proof (0:20)
Here is the kernel from our submission. Each thread finds its pixel from blockIdx times blockDim plus threadIdx, and a guard drops threads past the edge. It applies R transpose to find the source pixel, so every output pixel has exactly one writer. The launch rounds the grid up, and we check the error straight after because launches are asynchronous.

SLIDE 6 (0:38)
We built one variant per CUDA feature. The 2D grid beat the 1D grid, 1.55 against 2.96 milliseconds, because the 1D version pays a divide and modulo per thread. Not the cache: a 128 by 1 block is just as fast. Against one CPU core the kernel is 328 times faster, but end to end only 29.6, because copies take 91 percent of the time, so Amdahl caps us at 32.6. At 128 gigabytes per second, 40 percent of peak, the kernel is memory bound.

SLIDE 7 (0:30)
GPUDirect Storage lets NVMe DMA straight into GPU memory. The CPU keeps only the control path through cuFile, so the bounce buffer in host RAM goes away. So for the company, yes, but only for the bulk pipeline, and only if frames are raw or decoded on the GPU with nvJPEG. It does not help one photo, JPEGs decoded on the CPU, or images already in RAM.

Hi, I'm Erwyna and this is Taabish. Taabish rotates large images on a GPU with CUDA, then I cover the ethics of scaling HPC for AI, and our limitations.

SLIDE 8, energy (0:15)
The IEA puts data centres at 415 terawatt hours in 2024, and about 1,200 by 2035, with GPU servers driving the growth.

SLIDE 9, chip vs grid (0:20)
Frontier and LUMI use the same MI250X nodes, and their GFLOPS per watt are almost equal. But LUMI runs on hydropower and heats part of a Finnish town. So a FLOP's carbon cost depends on the grid and heat reuse, not only the chip.

SLIDE 10, Malaysia (0:22)
Johor went from 10 megawatts to over a gigawatt in about three years, and data centres could use almost a third of Peninsular electricity by 2035, on a grid that is 79 percent fossil. One myth check: AI does not emit more CO2 than aviation today. That is a 2030 projection.

SLIDE 11, governance (0:35)
Where the GPUs are decides which country's law covers the data. In data-parallel training, nodes swap gradients every iteration, and gradients can leak training samples, so data residency becomes a scheduler placement rule. Security is dual-use: the same accelerators train a hospital or a military model, so the US limits exports, and since July 2025 Malaysia needs a permit for US AI chips. Fairness has a compute cost too: Malay is under 0.1 percent of web text.

SLIDE 12, compute divide (0:25)
Frontier training compute grows about five times a year, and the best academic model sits about three thousand times lower. Most academics have one to eight GPUs. NCI Gadi has 776 GPUs and demand near three times its allocation. And if only companies can train frontier models, only companies can audit them.

SLIDE 13, SCALE (0:35)
UNESCO, the OECD and Malaysia's AIGE agree on values, but give operators no number to report. The EU is closer: the AI Act flags models above ten to the twenty-five FLOP, and sites over 500 kilowatts must report PUE. So we propose SCALE, where each step is a Slurm setting and a reported number. Size right: stop adding GPUs when parallel efficiency drops. Carbon-aware: jobs that can wait days and be paused cut CO2 by up to 19 percent. Account every joule. Keep data lawful and local. Give fair-share access.

SLIDE 14, limitations (0:28)
Our limits: a one-core CPU baseline on a shared Colab VM, so our speed-ups belong to that setup, and no GDS test. Most model energy figures are estimates, and Google's fleet only saved 1 to 2 percent at peak-carbon hours. Next, we would run it on a GDS server and measure energy with Slurm. So: rotation is memory bound and PCIe sets the ceiling, and the cost of a FLOP depends on where and for whom it runs.
